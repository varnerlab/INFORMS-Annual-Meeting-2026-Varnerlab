\begin{frame}{Key takeaways}
  % the template sets list text to \normalsize; the approved text fits at 9 pt on a slightly tighter leading
  \setbeamerfont{itemize/enumerate body}{size=\fontsize{9}{10.5}\selectfont}%
  \begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item {\vnmedium Utility functions set the weights.} We compared seven weighting rules: four utility functions
      (Cobb-Douglas, CES on shares, CES on weights, Stone–Geary), minimum variance, the tangency portfolio, and a
      130/30 long-short Cobb-Douglas. Under a budget alone, the utility rules have a closed-form answer. With limits
      on single names, sectors, volatility and turnover, the problem stays convex and a standard solver handles it.
      In the real run, the stocks we picked mattered more than the rule: on one 33-stock basket the seven rules ended
      between \SectorLo{} and \SectorHi{} times the starting wealth, while different baskets ranged from \BasketLo{}
      to \BasketHi{}.
    \item {\vnmedium The MPC trigger decides when to rebalance.} After each rebalance we simulate 1,000 paths of the
      portfolio's value over the next 21 trading days. We rebalance early if the value leaves the 95\% band or falls
      8\% below its peak, and otherwise wait out the 21 days. In the real run that came to about \Rebalances{}
      rebalances in 419 trading days.
    \item {\vnmedium We tested the method on simulated markets.} A JumpHMM fitted to 2014–2024 data reproduces fat
      tails and weak autocorrelation, but its volatility clustering fades after a day, while in real markets it
      lasts weeks. We used it to simulate 978 markets: 600
      typical, 200 with jump bursts and 200 deep bear markets. Picking 13 stocks by γ beat 13 random stocks in
      \TypicalLo–\TypicalHi\% of typical markets. In bear markets, random picks did as well or better for most
      weighting rules.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
